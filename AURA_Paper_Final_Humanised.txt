[10 pt, Bold, Italic] Abstract —

Mental health problems among engineering students are growing at a rate that 
most colleges are simply not equipped to handle. Stress, burnout, and anxiety 
quietly build up over weeks before anyone notices — and by then, helping the 
student gets much harder. The systems most colleges use today, like keeping 
records in spreadsheets or relying on informal WhatsApp group chats, were never 
designed to catch these issues early. This paper introduces AURA (Adaptive 
Understanding and Response Architecture), a web-based platform we built to 
change this. AURA gives every student, proctor, HOD, and parent their own 
role-based dashboard that shows real wellness data in real time. A mood tracker 
and AI chatbot collect student inputs, and a backend algorithm computes a stress 
score from these signals. When a student's score spikes above their personal 
normal range — detected using Z-Score analysis — the system immediately sends 
alerts to their proctor via WebSocket, email, or SMS. A Google Gemini 2.5 Flash 
chatbot handles empathetic mental conversations and academic assistance, 
including reading PDFs and images. We tested AURA with 23 functional test cases 
across all four user roles and the system passed every one. The results show 
that early, automated intervention is possible without requiring colleges to hire 
more staff — the system does the heavy lifting.

[SECTION I]
I. INTRODUCTION

Ask any engineering student what the hardest part of college is, and the answer 
is rarely a specific subject. More often, it is the relentless pressure — of 
deadlines, of competitive peers, of feeling like they are falling behind while 
everyone else seems fine. Research consistently shows that engineering students 
face higher rates of anxiety and burnout than students in other fields [5], yet 
colleges continue to address this with the same outdated tools: a counsellor 
who is available Tuesday mornings, a proctor who checks attendance, and a group 
chat that nobody monitors systematically.

What makes this especially frustrating is that the warning signs are usually 
there, just scattered and invisible. A student might stop engaging in class 
three weeks before a breakdown; another might start sending worried messages 
late at night. None of this gets captured in any system. By the time a faculty 
member notices something is wrong, the crisis has already arrived.

Our goal with AURA was straightforward: build a system that sees these signals 
in real time and acts on them before they become emergencies. The platform 
collects mood inputs and chatbot interaction data from students, converts them 
into a composite stress score using a weighted multi-signal algorithm, and uses 
Z-Score anomaly detection to spot unusual spikes against each student's personal 
21-day baseline. The moment a student's score crosses a risk threshold, their 
assigned proctor is notified automatically — no manual checking required.

Beyond monitoring, AURA provides students with a Gemini AI-powered chatbot for 
mental health conversations and academic study assistance. Proctors get a 
watchlist with 7-day trend sparklines. HODs see department-wide analytics. 
Parents receive SMS alerts for critical events. Everyone sees what they need, 
and nothing more. The result is a platform that shifts institutional wellness 
management from reactive to proactive.

[SECTION II]
II. LITERATURE SURVEY

The idea of using computers to track and respond to human emotions was first 
formalised by Picard [8], whose work on affective computing laid the groundwork 
for everything that followed. Calvo and D'Mello later built a comprehensive 
review of affect detection models and found that combining multiple data streams 
(language, behaviour, physiology) consistently outperformed single-signal 
approaches [9]. This directly shaped how we designed AURA's multi-signal 
stress computation.

On the language side, the BERT model introduced by Devlin et al. [1] showed 
that bidirectional transformers could understand context in ways that older NLP 
systems could not. Vaswani et al.'s attention mechanism [3] sits at the core of 
every large language model in use today, including the Gemini API that AURA 
uses. Sanford et al. [10] specifically reviewed AI chatbot interventions in 
mental health settings and found that structured, empathetic responses led to 
real improvements in student-reported wellbeing. Abd-Alrazaq et al. [11] went 
further and identified the features that actually matter in mental health 
chatbots: contextual memory, empathy, and a clear crisis escalation path.

Closer to our specific problem, Wahab et al. [4] demonstrated that NLP features 
extracted from student messages correlate reliably with their self-reported 
stress levels — meaning the system does not need students to explicitly say 
"I am stressed" to detect it. Sharma et al. [12] extended this by connecting 
keyword-based crisis detection to institutional intervention workflows. Gomes 
et al. [13] and Gupta et al. [6] provided the dashboard and real-time 
communication foundations on which AURA's alert delivery is built.

What is missing from the existing body of work is a single deployable platform 
that combines all of these elements — multi-stakeholder monitoring, AI chatbot 
support, sentiment analysis, and automated escalation — in a way that works on 
a real campus out of the box. AURA is built to be that platform.

[SECTION III]
III. EXISTING AND PROPOSED SYSTEM

A. Existing System

The way most engineering colleges currently handle student mental health is a 
patchwork of tools and habits that were never designed to work together. Proctors 
keep paper or Excel records of attendance. Grievances surface in WhatsApp 
messages that get buried quickly. Counselling is scheduled weeks in advance 
rather than triggered by actual need. There is no shared view of how a student 
is doing over time, no way to compare one student to another within a cohort, 
and no alert mechanism that fires automatically when something goes wrong.

The result is that institutions are always reacting — finding out about a 
problem after it has already caused visible damage. The student who failed three 
subjects and disappeared from campus for a week did not give no warning signals; 
those signals simply went unrecorded and unread.

B. Proposed System: AURA

AURA replaces this patchwork with a single integrated platform. Every student 
interaction — a mood selection, a chatbot conversation, a grievance submission — 
feeds into the same backend system, which continuously recomputes the student's 
stress score and risk tier. Role-based dashboards ensure that each type of 
user sees exactly the information they need:

  • Students see their own stress trends, mood history, wellness tips, and can 
    chat with the AI for support or academic help.
  • Proctors see their assigned students sorted by risk level, with 7-day stress 
    sparklines and real-time alerts when a student crosses the HIGH_RISK threshold.
  • HODs see aggregated department metrics, including how many students are at 
    each risk tier and how the department's average stress is trending over 30 days.
  • Parents see a simplified summary of their child's wellness and receive SMS 
    alerts for CRITICAL_RISK events.

The most important shift is from passive to active: instead of waiting for a 
student to reach out, AURA reaches out to the proctor first.

IV. METHODOLOGY

A. System Architecture

We structured AURA into five layers, each responsible for a distinct function.

Layer 1 — Authentication and Session Management: User credentials are validated 
against bcrypt-hashed records in MongoDB. Flask-Limiter enforces a five-attempt 
login rate limit on each IP address. All state-modifying routes require a CSRF 
token. Session cookies are HTTP-Only with a 60-minute lifetime.

Layer 2 — NLP-Based Emotional Processing: Every chatbot message is analysed by 
a sentiment pipeline that identifies mood category (happy, anxious, stressed, 
etc.), sentiment polarity, stress score indicators, and crisis keyword flags. 
Extracted data is stored against the student's email and timestamp for 
longitudinal tracking.

Layer 3 — Student Dashboard and Wellness Tracking: This layer renders the 
student's personal dashboard — their live stress gauge, 7-day mood history, 
wellness recommendations, and emotion-aware UI theme. When the system detects 
a student is anxious, the interface darkens and shifts to calm colours; when 
they are well, it brightens.

Figure 2 shows the student dashboard displaying a real-time stress score of 
11/100 (Relaxed) alongside quick-access modules for Mental Chat, Study Chat, 
Activities, and Relax features.

Fig. 2. Student Dashboard — Real-Time Stress Analysis and Quick Actions

Layer 4 — Risk Analysis Engine: The composite stress score is computed as:

  Stress Score = (Mood Weight × 0.4) + (Sentiment Score × 0.3) + (Activity Level × 0.3)

The Z-Score algorithm then compares this score to the student's 21-day personal 
baseline. A score more than 1.8 standard deviations above the baseline triggers 
a personalized anomaly flag, irrespective of the absolute score value.

Layer 5 — Institutional Monitoring and Alerts: Proctor, HOD, and Parent 
dashboards are updated in real time via Flask-SocketIO WebSocket connections. 
For HIGH_RISK events, an institutional email is sent. For CRITICAL_RISK events, 
Fast2SMS dispatches an SMS to the student's registered parent.

B. Risk Classification Algorithm

Every incoming stress score passes through a four-tier decision tree:

  1) CRITICAL_RISK — Crisis keywords detected (e.g., "can't go on", "give up")
     in English, Hindi/Hinglish, or Spanish. Triggers an immediate emergency 
     alert to the proctor and parent.
  2) HIGH_RISK — Stress score > 80 OR Z-Score anomaly detected. Triggers a 
     real-time proctor dashboard alert.
  3) MODERATE_RISK — Elevated stress consistent with baseline. Passive monitoring 
     continues, no alert dispatched.
  4) LOW_RISK — Normal behavioral patterns. No action required.

D. Parent Portal

The Parent Portal (Figure 5) is intentionally simple. It shows the student's 
average stress score (9.2/100), average mood, activity count, a 7-day stress 
trend graph, a mood distribution chart, and an academic performance summary. 
Parents are not shown raw chat logs — only aggregated outcomes, preserving 
student privacy while keeping families informed.

Fig. 5. Parent Portal — Student Wellness Overview and Stress Trend

E. AI Chatbot and Multimodal Study Assistant

Google Gemini 2.5 Flash serves as the primary AI engine for both the mental 
wellness chatbot and the study assistant. If Gemini is unavailable, the system 
automatically falls back to Groq (Llama 3.3 70B) and then OpenAI GPT. In 
study mode, students can upload PDFs or images and ask questions about the 
content — Gemini processes the files and returns contextual explanations, 
summaries, or practice questions.

V. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

A. Validation Experiments

We tested AURA through six categories of validation. In workflow testing, all 
four user roles completed their full interaction cycle successfully: students 
submitted moods and chatted with the AI, proctors received alerts, HODs 
reviewed department reports, and parents checked wellness summaries. The entire 
lifecycle, from mood input to proctor alert, completed in under two seconds in 
our test environment.

Dashboard validation confirmed that the RBAC decorator infrastructure correctly 
blocked all unauthorised cross-role access attempts — a student accessing 
/proctor/dashboard received a 403 response without exception.

NLP testing compared the system's extracted sentiment labels against a manually 
annotated set of 50 sample student messages. The rule-based classification 
pipeline consistently mapped message content to the correct mood category, with 
errors occurring only on highly ambiguous messages containing sarcasm or 
contradictory signals.

Risk analysis validation populated the database with synthetic stress profiles 
at each risk tier. The Z-Score engine classified each profile correctly, and 
WebSocket alerts reached the proctor dashboard within milliseconds of a tier 
transition.

Usability testing with actual undergraduate students and faculty showed that 
users found the dashboards intuitive without training. Proctors in particular 
noted that having a sorted watchlist with 7-day sparklines let them identify 
at-risk students in seconds rather than having to scroll through spreadsheets 
and compose emails.

VI. FUTURE SCOPE

AURA in its current form is a working prototype that addresses the immediate 
problem of reactive wellness monitoring. The next phase of development would 
push it toward genuine prediction and deeper integration.

The most impactful next step is building a predictive model — training a 
classifier on AURA's accumulated longitudinal stress and mood data to forecast 
which students are likely to deteriorate in the coming week, before their scores 
breach any threshold. Random Forests [21] and gradient boosting are natural 
candidates given the tabular nature of the data.

On the infrastructure side, moving from a monolithic Flask application to a 
microservices architecture would allow the platform to scale to multi-campus 
deployments. A companion mobile app would remove the dependency on campus 
computers for mood logging, which we observed was a friction point during 
usability testing.

Wearable integration is a longer-term direction that we believe would 
significantly improve signal quality. Heart rate variability, sleep tracking, 
and step counts from consumer smartwatches are physiological correlates of 
stress that would complement the behavioral signals AURA currently collects.

Finally, adding OAuth 2.0 single sign-on via the institution's identity 
provider would remove the need for separate registration, reducing the barrier 
to adoption. Blockchain-based audit trails for sensitive mental health records 
would address privacy compliance requirements that are increasingly being 
enforced by regulatory bodies.

[SECTION VII]
VII. CONCLUSION

AURA started from a simple observation: the tools that most colleges use to 
monitor student mental health were designed for a different era, and they are 
not working. WhatsApp messages get lost, spreadsheets go unread, and counselling 
sessions fill up before students who actually need them get an appointment.

We built AURA to address this directly — not with a research prototype that 
requires a specialist to operate, but with a deployable web application that 
slots into an existing institutional hierarchy. Students keep using the 
interface they already navigate; proctors and HODs get dashboards that give 
them the information they need, in the order they need it. The AI does the 
continuous monitoring that no human staff member has the bandwidth to do.

The 23-test validation demonstrated that the technical foundation is sound. 
The usability feedback confirmed that the design is intuitive. Every component, 
from the Z-Score anomaly engine to the Gemini chatbot fallback chain, was built 
to be reliable in conditions that are unpredictable — because student mental 
health crises do not schedule themselves conveniently.

We believe AURA is a meaningful contribution to the growing field of AI-enabled 
academic wellness support, and its architecture is deliberately extensible for 
the predictive and mobile capabilities that will define the next generation of 
institutional health platforms.
