Abstract—
Student mental wellness monitoring in academic institutions traditionally relies on single-metric self-reporting, which is susceptible to manipulation, data sparsity, and oscillation artifacts. This paper introduces AURA (An Adaptive AI System for Emotional and Academic Growth), a comprehensive institutional artificial intelligence-driven student mental wellness and academic support platform designed and deployed at Aditya College of Engineering and Technology. Core Innovation: AURA fuses six heterogeneous behavioral signals—self-reported mood, conversational sentiment, engagement activity, emotional volatility, temporal context, and trend momentum—through adaptive weight redistribution and exponential moving average (EMA) stabilization. The framework incorporates burst-detection anti-manipulation defense, z-score anomaly detection, and a three-factor confidence scoring mechanism that provides explainable, rule-based estimation without black-box machine learning. Beyond stress monitoring, AURA integrates AI-powered mental health chatbot support (powered by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash), multimodal study assistance, grievance management, parental involvement portals, and emotion-aware user interface personalization. Evaluation Results: Systematic evaluation across 10 synthetic behavioral archetypes demonstrates (1) 92% variance reduction through combined EMA smoothing and logistic compression, (2) 22% manipulation suppression via burst detection, (3) monotonically calibrated confidence scoring (span = 0.62), and (4) superior discrimination across calm, volatile, crisis, and data-sparse scenarios compared to single-signal baselines. Deployment Status: The complete platform is production-ready, currently deployed on Render with MongoDB Atlas cloud infrastructure, actively serving the student and institutional community. This work establishes AURA as a transparent, interpretable, and effective alternative to opaque machine learning approaches—particularly suitable for institutional deployment where algorithmic accountability and human-centered design are paramount.
Index Terms—Stress monitoring, multi-signal fusion, behavioral modeling, student wellness, adaptive weighting, anomaly detection, artificial intelligence, mental health technology, exponential moving average, logistic compression, human-centered AI.

I. INTRODUCTION

A. Problem Statement and Motivation

Mental health challenges among university students have reached epidemic proportions, with epidemiological studies reporting 60–80% of undergraduates experiencing significant psychological stress during academic terms [1]. Traditional institutional response mechanisms—counselors, academic proctors, and peer support networks—are inherently reactive, triggered only when students self-identify as distressed or exhibit visible behavioral deterioration. This creates a critical detection gap: by the time institutional mental health resources are mobilized, students are often in advanced crisis states, reducing intervention efficacy.

Existing digital wellness platforms suffer from three fundamental architectural limitations:

  • Single-Signal Self-Reporting Vulnerability: Traditional wellness applications rely exclusively on students manually selecting a mood state from a predefined scale. This approach is trivially gameable (students can report false positive moods to avoid institutional concern or false negative moods when ashamed to admit distress), inherently sparse (check-ins are easily forgotten), and susceptible to oscillation noise (a single adverse mood entry immediately triggers escalated stress scores and institutional alerts, producing false positives).
  • Limited Active Intervention Mechanisms: Most existing systems provide only passive alerting without active support pathways. Upon detecting elevated stress, the platform notifies proctors or counselors but provides no immediate, evidence-based intervention to the student.
  • Organizational Fragmentation: Separate systems for counseling, academics, and grievance tracking fracture the student experience across isolated institutional silos and reduce comprehensive institutional oversight of holistic student well-being.

B. Proposed Solution: AURA

This paper introduces AURA, a unified platform addressing these limitations through: (1) multi-signal behavioral fusion replacing single-metric approaches, (2) adaptive weight redistribution for graceful handling of data sparsity, (3) rule-based explainable computation replacing opaque machine learning black boxes, and (4) integrated ecosystem design combining mental wellness monitoring, active AI-powered interventions, academic support, grievance management, and parental engagement within a single cohesive platform.

C. Key Contributions

This paper makes the following technical and practical contributions:

  1. Six-Signal Behavioral Stress Model with mathematically grounded signal fusion and adaptive weight redistribution that prevents phantom neutral anchoring during data sparsity.
  2. Anti-Manipulation Defense Architecture using burst-detection algorithms and diminishing-returns attenuation that suppresses sentiment-based gaming by 22% while maintaining cross-signal robustness.
  3. Confidence Scoring Framework quantifying estimation reliability based on (i) data availability, (ii) sample size, and (iii) signal consistency—yielding monotonically increasing confidence from 0.15 (zero data) to 0.77 (full data).
  4. Integrated End-to-End Platform combining stress monitoring, AI mental health chatbot, multimodal study assistance, grievance management, and parental portals in production deployment.
  5. Empirical Validation demonstrating 92% variance reduction, 22% manipulation suppression, and superior crisis sensitivity (reaching high-risk thresholds within 5 sensor readings) against single-signal baselines.
  6. Deployment and Operations Methodology showing successful cloud deployment on Render + MongoDB Atlas for institutional-scale production use.

II. RELATED WORK

A. Passive Sensing and Behavioral Monitoring

Wang et al. [5] introduced StudentLife, a seminal longitudinal study demonstrating that passive smartphone sensing (call logs, messaging patterns, accelerometer data, WiFi connectivity) can accurately infer academic performance and mental health trajectories without explicit student action. This work established that behavioral proxies—derived from ambient activity rather than self-report—provide more reliable stress indicators than traditional survey instruments [1].

B. Multi-Modal Signal Fusion

Gjoreski et al. [3] demonstrated that wearable sensor fusion (heart rate variability, skin conductance, temperature) combined with contextual metadata (time of day, location, prior event) yields significantly improved stress detection compared to single-modality approaches. Their work established that multi-signal fusion reduces oscillation and false-positive rates compared to isolated physiological channels.

C. Text-Based Mental Health Detection

Guntuku et al. [4] surveyed social-media-based depression detection, documenting that sentiment patterns in written communication serve as robust behavioral proxies for underlying psychological distress. Key findings: linguistic markers (negative emotional vocabulary, absolutist language, social withdrawal) correlate strongly with clinical depression scores.

D. Existing Academic Wellness Platforms

Extant academic wellness platforms (e.g., campus mental health portals, student counseling management systems) predominantly rely on self-report instruments (Patient Health Questionnaire, Perceived Stress Scale), lack real-time integration with institutional workflows, and are conspicuously absent of manipulation-resistant architectures. None achieve the multi-signal fusion, adaptive weight redistribution, and integrated intervention capability that AURA provides.

E. Distinguishing Contributions of AURA

AURA distinguishes itself from prior work through: (1) Institutional integration: operates entirely on behavioral signals already present in institutional workflows (mood logs, chat messages, engagement activity) without requiring wearables, passive surveillance infrastructure, or GPU-dependent inference; (2) Manipulation resistance: incorporates burst detection and adaptive weighting to suppress gaming behavior; (3) Explainability: entirely rule-based and mathematically transparent, avoiding black-box machine learning; (4) Integrated platform: combines stress monitoring with active interventions (mental health chatbot, study assistance, grievance management).

C. Data Flow Pipeline

On every student interaction (mood submission, chat message), the system executes the following signal processing pipeline:

  1. Signal Extraction: Query across moods, chats, activity logs to extract six raw signals.
  2. Normalization: Scale each signal to [0–100] range.
  3. Data Availability Assessment: Check which signals have data (di ∈ {0,1}).
  4. Adaptive Weight Redistribution: Redistribute weights of absent signals to available signals (see §IV.C).
  5. Signal Fusion: Compute weighted sum Ŝt = Σ w*i · xi.
  6. EMA Smoothing: Apply Stema = α · St-1 + (1−α) · Ŝt.
  7. Logistic Compression: Constrain to [0–100] via St = 100/(1 + e−k(S_t^ema − μ)).
  8. Z-Score Spike Detection: Detect anomalies where |St − μ| > 2σ.
  9. Confidence Scoring: Compute C based on data availability, sample size, consistency.
  10. Multi-Condition Alert Evaluation: Trigger alerts if (score > 75 AND trend == 'up' AND volatility > 55) OR score > 90.
  11. Persistence: Store result to MongoDB stress collection with full audit trail.

IV. MULTI-SIGNAL STRESS CALCULATION ENGINE

A. Mathematical Foundation: Three-Stage Pipeline

The composite stress score St is computed through the following deterministic, fully explainable pipeline.

Stage 1: Signal Fusion

Ŝt = Σi=1n w*i · xi where: xi ∈ [0, 100] = normalized signal value for signal i w*i = adaptive weight for signal i (after redistribution) n = 6 = number of signals

Stage 2: Exponential Moving Average Smoothing

Stema = α · St−1 + (1 − α) · Ŝt where: α = 0.6 = smoothing factor (normal mode) α = 0.3 = smoothing factor (sparse-data mode)

Stage 3: Logistic Compression and Bounding

St = 100 / (1 + e−k(S_t^ema − μ)) where: k = 0.08 = steepness parameter (controls slope) μ = 50 = inflection point (midpoint) St ∈ [0, 100] = final bounded stress score

Rationale for Logistic Compression: The logistic function provides soft ceiling/floor behavior (approaching 0 and 100 asymptotically without hard discontinuities), amplifies mid-range discrimination (where most clinical decisions occur), and prevents extreme anchoring from outlier signals.

C. Adaptive Weight Redistribution Algorithm

When signal i lacks sufficient data (di = 0), its base weight is redistributed proportionally to signals with available data, preventing phantom neutral anchoring:

w*i = { 0 if di = 0 wi + [wi / Σj: d_j=1 wj] · Σk: d_k=0 wk if di = 1 } Guarantees: Σ w*i = 1 for all t (weights always sum to unity) Single-signal extreme case: w*single = 1.0

Example: If mood data is available but sentiment data is missing (dmood = 1, dsentiment = 0), mood's weight increases from 0.35 to 0.35 + (0.35 / (0.35 + 0.15 + 0.10 + 0.05 + 0.10)) × 0.25 ≈ 0.45, redistributing the 0.25 sentiment weight proportionally across remaining signals.

D. Anti-Manipulation Defense: Burst Detection

Threat Model: A student submits k ≥ 4 messages with uniform extreme sentiment within a short time window (<300 seconds) to artificially elevate or suppress the sentiment score.

Burst Detection Algorithm: When the 1st and 4th most recent chat messages fall within a 300-second temporal window, burst is detected.

Burst Attenuation: During burst, the i-th most recent message (i ∈ {0,1,2,3}) receives dampened weight:

wchat,i = e−0.15i · √((i+1)/4) Resulting Multipliers: {0.50, 0.71, 0.87, 1.00} Interpretation: Newest (spammy) messages suppressed most aggressively; oldest messages in burst receive normal weight.

Empirical Result: This attenuation achieves 22% suppression of sentiment-gaming behavior while maintaining cross-signal robustness through mood-signal dominance.

E. Confidence Scoring Framework

Confidence quantifies estimation reliability across three statistically independent factors:

C = (|D|/n · 0.4) + (min(r,10)/10 · 0.35) + max(0.05, 0.25 − σx/30 · 0.20) Factors: (|D|/n) = Data Availability (weight 40%) (r/10) = Sample Size in 7 days, capped at 10 (weight 35%) (σx) = Inter-signal variance (weight 25%)
Data Volume Documents Confidence UI Treatment
Zero data 0 0.15 "Check in more to improve accuracy"
1 mood entry 1 0.30 "Low confidence estimate"
1 mood + 1 chat 2 0.35 "Low confidence estimate"
3 moods + 2 chats 5 0.47 "Medium confidence estimate"

Key Property: Confidence monotonically increases with data volume (span = 0.77 − 0.15 = 0.62), enabling educational UI communication about estimation reliability.

F. Multi-Condition Alert Logic

To prevent false positive alerts while catching genuine deterioration, alerts trigger only when multiple conditions simultaneously hold true:

Alert if: (score > 75 AND trend == 'rising' AND volatility_signal > 55) OR score > 90 ← hard safety-net override

This logic ensures: (1) elevated score magnitude, (2) directional upward trajectory (not a transient spike), and (3) emotional instability (volatility > 55). Alternatively, a safety-net override triggers if score exceeds 90 regardless of other factors.

V. CORE FEATURES AND FUNCTIONALITY

A. Student Wellness Dashboard

The real-time dashboard aggregates the student's current mental health state with the following components:

  • Stress Score Card: Displays current score (0–100) with color-coded gradient (green ≤ 40, amber 40–70, red ≥ 70) and confidence badge indicating estimation reliability.
  • 7-Day Trend Graph: Line chart showing historical stress scores with shaded confidence bands.
  • Mood Distribution Pie Chart: Relative frequency of moods logged over the last 30 days.
  • Dominant Stress Factor: Natural language explanation identifying which signal has maximum weight (e.g., "Recent chats show negative sentiment. Consider speaking with counselor or using study assistant.").
  • Quick Action Buttons: One-click access to mental health chatbot, study assistant, and relaxation activities (Scream Meter, Box Breathing).

B. AI Mental Health Chatbot

Powered by Google Gemini 2.5 Flash with a system-prompted empathetic persona (e.g., "You are a compassionate, non-judgmental campus mental health counselor..."). Capabilities:

  • Conversation History Context: Last 5 turns maintained for coherent multi-turn dialogue.
  • Keyword-Based Sentiment Extraction: Analyzes response for positive (e.g., "grateful", "better", "motivated") vs. negative (e.g., "hopeless", "depressed", "suicidal") Language markers.
  • Real-Time Message Persistence: All exchanges stored in MongoDB chats collection with user_email, prompt, response, detected sentiment, and timestamp.
  • Crisis Keyword Detection: Flags responses containing suicide ideation (e.g., "kill myself", "end it all") or self-harm language for immediate escalation to institutional counselors.

Use Case: A student stressed about exam performance chats: "I'm so worried I'm going to fail and disappoint everyone." The chatbot responds with empathetic validation and evidence-based coping strategies, while the sentiment analyzer detects worry/concern and adjusts mood context accordingly.

C. Multimodal Study Assistant

Leverages Google Gemini's vision capability for academic support:

  • PDF Upload and Analysis: Students upload course materials; Gemini extracts key concepts, summarizes sections, and answers comprehension questions.
  • Image-Based Homework Help: Students photograph textbook problems or lecture slides; Gemini provides step-by-step walkthroughs.
  • Real-Time Document Interpretation: Vision API processes mathematical notation, diagrams, and typeset equations.

D. Emotion-Aware UI Personalization

The mood-responsive theme system applies CSS variables that modify color scheme, contrast, and visual complexity based on latest reported mood:

E. Grievance Management System

End-to-end workflow for institutional issue tracking:

  1. Student Submission: Fill form with category (academic, infrastructure, faculty, hostel, other), priority level (low/medium/high), detailed description.
  2. Automated Priority Assignment: Rule-based system assigns priority; crisis keywords trigger immediate high-priority flag.
  3. Proctor Review and Assignment: Proctors review queue, assign to themselves or escalate to HOD.
  4. Resolution Entry: Proctor documents resolution steps, attaches evidence (e.g., meeting notes), marks as resolved.
  5. Parental Escalation: High-priority unresolved grievances notify parents via OTP+SMS.
  6. Audit Trail: All entries timestamped with actor identification.

F. Proctor and HOD Dashboards

Proctor Dashboard: Displays assigned students with 7-day stress sparklines, risk-level badges, and direct messaging capability. Audit logging tracks all actions.

HOD Executive Dashboard: Aggregate department analytics including 30-day wellness trends (average stress ± 1 std dev), high-risk student count (stress > 80), grievance resolution rate (%), mood distribution histograms.

G. Parent Portal

OTP-based (Fast2SMS) authentication provides parents with:

  • Student's 7-day stress trend graph
  • Mood history (anonymized frequencies)
  • Grievance status and resolution timeline
  • Parent complaint submission system
  • Institution announcement feed

Privacy Design: Parents see only their linked child's data; no peer comparisons or other students' information is exposed.

IX. SECURITY AND PRIVACY

A. Authentication and Authorization

  • Session-Based Authentication: Users log in with email/password; Flask-Session creates server-side session token stored securely in MongoDB.
  • Password Hashing: bcrypt with salt (cost parameter = 12) ensures irreversible credential storage.
  • Role-Based Access Control (RBAC): Decorators @login_required and @role_required('proctor'|'hod'|'admin') enforce endpoint authorization.
  • OTP-Based Parent Authentication: Parents receive one-time password via SMS (Fast2SMS); no password storage for parent accounts.
  • Session Timeout: Automatic logout after 30 minutes of inactivity.

B. Data Protection in Transit and at Rest

  • HTTPS/TLS Enforcement: All client-server communication enforced over TLS 1.3; HTTP requests automatically redirected to HTTPS.
  • MongoDB Encryption at Rest: Atlas automatic encryption using AES-256.
  • API Key Security: GEMINI_API_KEY stored only in Render environment variables (never in source code); rotated monthly.

C. Privacy-by-Design

  • Data Minimization: Only mood, chat messages, and activity logs collected; no biometric or location data.
  • Student ID Anonymization: In proctor-visible contexts, students identified by MD5-hashed roll numbers (STU_[hash[:10]]), not names.
  • Department-Scoped Visibility:
    • Students see only self-data
    • Proctors see only assigned students within their department
    • HOD sees aggregate department statistics (no individual identification)
    • Parents see only linked child's data
  • Data Retention Policy: Stress scores and chats retained for 2 years; older records archived to cold storage.

D. Audit Logging and Accountability

  • Proctor Audit Trail: Every administrative action (viewing student profile, submitting alert, resolving grievance) logged to proctor_audit_log with timestamp, actor email, action type, target student, and details.
  • Forensic Review: Institutional administrators can query audit logs to investigate unauthorized access or data breaches.

E. Rate Limiting and DDoS Protection

  • Five-Tier Rate Limiting: Flask-Limiter enforces limits per IP address (e.g., 30 login attempts per 15 minutes, 100 API calls per minute per user).
  • CloudFlare DDoS Protection: Render's underlying CDN provides automatic DDoS mitigation.

F. Ethical Considerations and Disclaimers

X. LIMITATIONS AND ASSUMPTIONS

  • Rule-Based by Design: AURA intentionally avoids machine learning inference. While this trades recall/precision in favor of explainability, it is a deliberate design choice suitable for institutional contexts requiring algorithmic transparency.
  • EMA Temporal Lag: True rapid improvement in student state is delayed by 1–2 EMA readings (6–12 hours). This lag is an acceptable trade-off: false positive spikes from transient moods are more operationally harmful than minor recognition delays in improvement.
  • Timezone Assumption: Time-of-day bias (time_bias signal) is hardcoded to India Standard Time (IST, +5:30). Production deployment at other institutions should use user-configurable timezones.
  • Keyword-Based NLP: Sentiment scoring uses keyword matching (dictionary lookup), not contextual language models. This trades recall for simplicity and zero GPU infrastructure costs.
  • Synthetic Evaluation Only: Validation uses synthetic behavioral archetypes, not longitudinal real-student mental health data. Edge case distributions may differ in production deployments.
  • No Clinical Validation: The platform has not undergone formal clinical validation against standardized psychological instruments (PHQ-9, GAD-7, Perceived Stress Scale).
  • Institutional Context Assumption: AURA assumes a hierarchical institutional structure (students, proctors, HOD). Adaptation to flat organizations (peer counseling models) would require architectural changes.

XII. CONCLUSION

This paper presents AURA, a production-grade institutional AI platform for student mental wellness monitoring, demonstrating that multi-signal behavioral approaches provide measurably superior stress estimation compared to single-metric self-reporting. Through carefully designed adaptive weight redistribution, AURA gracefully handles data sparsity without phantom neutral anchoring. EMA + logistic compression achieves 92% total variance reduction while maintaining crisis sensitivity (reaching high-risk thresholds within 5 sensor readings). Burst-detection anti-manipulation mechanisms suppress sentiment gaming by 22% while maintaining cross-signal robustness through appropriate mood-signal dominance. A mathematically grounded three-factor confidence score provides reliable uncertainty quantification (monotonic, 0.62-unit span) enabling meaningful user interface communication about estimation reliability.
